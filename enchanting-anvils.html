Enchanting & Anvils

Enchanting & Anvils

Hard

Enchanting turns ordinary gear into equipment worth keeping, and the anvil is how you combine, repair and rename it. Both cost experience, and both punish careless ordering, so understanding the rules saves a great deal of grinding.

The Enchanting Table
An enchanting table needs two things to work: lapis lazuli, which is consumed, and experience levels, which are spent. On its own the table offers only weak enchantments. Surrounding it with bookshelves raises the levels it can offer.

Bookshelves
Fifteen bookshelves is the maximum that has any effect, and that is what unlocks level 30 enchantments. Placement matters: each bookshelf must sit one block away from the table with an air gap between, either level with the table or one block higher. Blocking that gap with a torch or a slab stops the bookshelf counting, which is the usual reason a table refuses to offer level 30.

How the Offers Work
The table presents three options at once. The enchantments you are shown are tied to a seed that only rerolls after you take one, so if none of the three appeal, enchanting a cheap item such as a wooden sword will reroll the list. Taking the lowest option costs less experience but tends to give fewer and weaker enchantments.

The Anvil
An anvil does three jobs: it combines two of the same item, it applies an enchanted book to an item, and it renames things. It also repairs gear using either matching material or a second copy of the item.
Every operation costs experience levels, and in survival mode any operation priced at 40 levels or more is refused outright as too expensive. This is the wall most players hit when stacking enchantments.

Prior Work Penalty
This is the rule that catches people out. Each time an item passes through an anvil, its prior work penalty doubles the cost of every future anvil operation on it, regardless of what you are doing. An item repaired and renamed several times can become impossible to enchant further, even though nothing about the enchantment itself changed.
The way around it is ordering. Combine your enchanted books with each other first, pairing them up and then pairing the results, before touching the actual tool. Applying one finished book to a fresh item is dramatically cheaper than applying six books to it one at a time.

Anvils Wear Out
Anvils are not permanent. Each use carries roughly a 12 percent chance of damaging the anvil, which passes through three stages before it breaks apart entirely. Keep iron on hand if you intend to do serious enchanting work.

The Grindstone
A grindstone strips enchantments from an item and refunds some experience, which makes it useful for clearing a bad enchantment or resetting villager-traded gear. It does not remove curses. It also clears the prior work penalty, so a heavily worked item can be salvaged, at the cost of everything enchanted on it.

Conflicts and Curses
Some enchantments cannot coexist. Silk Touch and Fortune are mutually exclusive, as are Mending and Infinity on a bow, and the various damage enchantments on a sword.
Curse of Binding prevents you removing a piece of armour once worn. Curse of Vanishing destroys the item on death. Neither can be removed with a grindstone, so check traded and looted gear before equipping it.

Practical Tips
Build a proper enchanting setup with all 15 bookshelves before spending levels; partial setups waste experience.
Do your book combining first and your item enchanting last.
Rename items at the same time as another operation rather than as a separate anvil use, to avoid an extra prior work penalty.
Mending is the single most valuable enchantment for long-term gear, since it repairs equipment from experience you collect anyway.

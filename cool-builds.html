Cool Builds

Cool Builds

Guide

Most builds do not fail because the builder lacked ideas. They fail because a structure that looked right while it was going up reads as flat, thin and oddly small once it is finished and you walk away from it. Almost every fix for that comes down to four things: palette, depth, scale and setting.

Why Flat Builds Look Flat
A box built from a single block type, three or four blocks tall, with a hole cut in one side for a door, is the default output of a first build. It looks wrong for reasons that are easy to name once you see them. The surface has no variation, so the eye has nothing to catch on. Every wall sits on a single flat plane, so nothing casts a shadow. And the whole thing is too small, because a room that feels roomy while you are standing in it looks like a shed from thirty blocks away.
Palette fixes the first of those, depth fixes the second, and scale fixes the third. Setting, meaning how the build meets the ground around it, is what the later sections are for.

Building a Palette
Pick three to five blocks and commit to them. More than that and the build turns to noise; fewer and it reads as a texture sample rather than a structure.
A workable palette has a base, a secondary, a frame and an accent. Stone bricks as the base, cobblestone or andesite as the secondary, dark oak as the frame, and something with colour for the accent. The base carries most of the surface, the secondary breaks it up, the frame draws the edges, and the accent appears rarely enough to still be worth noticing.
The useful trick is that the base and secondary should be close in tone. Stone, cobblestone, andesite and gravel all sit in the same grey family, so mixing them produces texture without producing stripes. Contrast belongs on the frame and the accent, not scattered across the wall.

Depth Is the Whole Game
A flat wall has no shadows. Give it any variation in the third dimension and the game lights it for you.
Inset the windows by a block so they sit in a recess. Push the supporting pillars out by a block so they cast a line down the facade. Set the upper storey slightly forward of the lower one so it overhangs. Break a long wall with a section that steps back, rather than running the whole length flat.
Stairs and slabs are the tools for this at small scale. Upside-down stairs under an overhang, slabs as a ledge halfway up a wall, and trapdoors laid flat against a surface all add a fraction of a block of relief, which is enough to catch light. A wall built from full blocks alone will always look like a wall built from full blocks alone.

Scale and Proportion
Build bigger than feels natural. The instinct is to make interiors efficient, and efficient interiors produce cramped exteriors.
Some rules of thumb that hold up: walls one block thick read as paper, so make the important ones two or three. Doorways two blocks wide look like entrances; one block wide looks like a gap. A ceiling at three blocks feels like a corridor, at five it feels like a room, and at seven it feels like a hall. If you are building something grand, the grandeur is almost entirely in the vertical measurement.
Height is also what separates a house from a tower and a tower from a landmark, and it costs only blocks.

Roofs
The roof is the part most often left as a flat slab, and it is the part most visible from a distance.
Stairs give you a pitch. A shallow pitch, one block of rise for every two of run, suits long buildings; a steep pitch, one for one or steeper, suits towers and anything you want to look tall. Let the roof overhang the wall by a block so the building has a shadow line under the eaves. Finish the ridge with a row of slabs or a different block so the peak is a deliberate edge rather than a seam.
A gable end, where the roof pitch is exposed at the short side of the building, is worth learning as a shape. It gives you a triangle to decorate, and it is the single detail that most reliably makes a build read as architecture rather than as a box.

Hide Your Light
Torches stuck at intervals along a wall are the fastest way to make a careful build look unfinished. Since a block light level above zero is all that stops hostile spawns, you have complete freedom in where the light comes from.
Put glowstone or sea lanterns under the floor and cover them with carpet, which lets the light through. Hide light sources behind open trapdoors, under slabs and stairs, inside the recess above a window, or in the gap under an overhanging roof. Lanterns hanging from chains are one of the few light sources that look deliberate in the open.
The goal is a lit building with no visible fixtures except the ones you chose to show.

Build Into the Landscape
Flattening a hill and putting a rectangle on the plate is the most common way to make an otherwise good build look wrong. The structure ends up sitting on the world rather than in it.
Instead, let the terrain do work. Step the foundation down a slope rather than levelling it. Cut into a hillside so the back of the building disappears into the rock. Bring the ground up against the walls with dirt and grass so there is no hard line where the build begins. Run a path away from the door and let it wander with the land.
Landscaping around a build usually improves it more, per block placed, than any additional detail on the build itself.

The Inside Counts
A large number of impressive exteriors are hollow. If the building is meant to be entered, the interior needs the same treatment: varied floor materials, a ceiling with visible beams rather than a flat lid, furniture assembled from stairs, slabs, trapdoors and item frames, and a reason for each room to exist.
Item frames and armour stands are the difference between a room and a room somebody lives in.

How to Actually Improve
Build it, then walk away and look at it from a distance, at different times of day. Most flaws in proportion are invisible from three blocks away and obvious from thirty.
Test palettes in a creative world before committing to them in survival. Copying a build you admire, block for block, teaches more than inventing something mediocre from scratch, because it forces you to notice choices you would not have made. And expect to demolish things: the fastest builders are usually the ones most willing to tear down a wall that is not working.

A Short Checklist
Three to five blocks in the palette, with the base and secondary close in tone.
No wall left flat: inset, outset, or step it.
Taller and wider than feels necessary.
A pitched roof with an overhang.
Every light source hidden.
The ground brought up to meet the building.
An interior worth walking into.
